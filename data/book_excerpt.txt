My father's family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip. I give Pirrip as my father's family name on the authority of his tombstone and my sister—Mrs. Joe Gargery, who married the blacksmith. As I never saw my father or my mother, and never saw any likeness of either of them (for their days were long before the days of photographs), my first fancies regarding what they were like were unreasonably derived from their tombstones. The shape of the letters on my father's grave, I have not the faintest idea which.

Ours was the marsh country, down by the river, within, as the river wound, twenty miles of the sea. My first most vivid and broad impression of the identity of things seems to me to have been gained on a memorable raw afternoon towards evening. At such a time I found out for certain that this bleak place overgrown with nettles was the churchyard; and that Philip Pirrip, late of this parish, and also Georgiana, wife of the above, were dead and buried; and that Alexander, Bartholomew, Abraham, Tobias, and Roger, infant children of the aforesaid, were also dead and buried; and that the dark flat wilderness beyond the churchyard, intersected with dykes and mounds and gates, with scattered cattle feeding on it, was the marshes; and that the low leaden line beyond was the river; and that the distant savage lair from which the wind was rushing was the sea.

"Hold your noise!" cried a terrible voice, as a man started up from among the graves at the side of the church porch. "Keep still, you little devil, or I'll cut your throat!" A fearful man, all in coarse gray, with a great iron on his leg. A man with no hat, and with broken shoes, and with an old rag tied round his head. A man who had been soaked in water, and smothered in mud, and lamed by stones, and cut by flints, and stung by nettles, and torn by briars; who limped, and shivered, and glared and growled; and whose teeth chattered in his head as he seized me by the chin.

"O! Don't cut my throat, sir," I pleaded in terror. "Pray don't do it, sir." "Tell us your name!" said the man. "Quick!" "Pip, sir." "Once more," said the man, staring at me. "Give it mouth!" "Pip. Pip, sir." "Show us where you live," said the man. "Point out the place!" I pointed to where our village lay, on the flat in-shore among the alder-trees and pollards, a mile or more from the church. The man, after looking at me for a moment, turned me upside down, and emptied my pockets. There was nothing in them but a piece of bread. When the church came to itself—for he was so sudden and strong that he made it go head over heels before me, and I staggered under the shock—I was seated on a high tombstone, trembling violently.

"You young dog," said the man, licking his lips, "what fat cheeks you ha' got." I have often thought since, and I think now, that this speech was a finer touch of realism in that man than many naturalists could command. It sent a chill down my spine; but at the same moment there was a sound like a distant gun, and, upon the instant, the man's face changed, and he turned his head in that direction. Something clicked in the distance, like the cocking of a gun. He swore an oath at that sound, and turned back to me with increased menace in his manner, pulling me toward him by my collar and looking into my face as if to learn something from its expression.

The apparition of a file of soldiers, in red coats and with their muskets at the ready, coming down the slope from the direction of the town, gave the man new fears. He put me down, and made off with great speed in the direction of the marshes. I did not attempt to follow him, but stood petrified with terror, my teeth chattering, my knees shaking, unable to do anything but stare after him as he disappeared into the mist and shadow. The soldiers passed near enough for me to see their faces clearly, and one of them called out, but the man was already lost to sight among the dykes and mounds of the marshes, as completely as if the earth had opened and swallowed him.

I made my way back towards our house with a heavy heart, carrying the image of that terrible man in my mind's eye. The marsh stretched out before me, desolate and gray beneath the darkening sky. In the distance, I could hear the cry of the curlews, a sound that only served to deepen my sense of isolation and unease. By the time I reached the gate of our cottage, I was thoroughly chilled and exhausted, and the events of that afternoon had impressed themselves so deeply upon my imagination that I knew I should never forget them. The fear that had taken hold of me refused to loosen its grip, and I went into the house scarcely daring to speak of what I had seen.

My sister, Mrs. Joe, was in a state of considerable exasperation when I came in, having been called away from her washing to give me a piece of her mind regarding my tardiness. She was a woman of about thirty, with a square figure and a tight apron, and when she was displeased, as she was now, her displeasure made itself known in no uncertain terms. She gave me a cuff on the side of my head, and demanded to know where I had been, and why I had taken so long in returning from the churchyard. I could find no words to explain myself, tongue-tied as I was by the shock of my recent encounter and by the usual terror of my sister's anger.

That night, as I lay in my small bed in the attic room of the cottage, I found myself unable to sleep. The darkness seemed to press in upon me from all sides, and every sound—the creak of a floorboard, the scratch of a branch against the window, the distant cry of an animal—sent fresh currents of fear through my already overwrought nerves. I thought of the man in the marshes, and of the file of soldiers, and of the strange mixture of terror and pity that I felt in my breast. What had brought such a desperate creature to that desolate place? What was he fleeing from, and what was it that he sought? These questions occupied my mind throughout the long hours of darkness, and by the time morning came, I had resolved to keep the secret of my encounter to myself.

As the years passed, and I grew from a boy into a young man, I often reflected upon those events of that memorable afternoon, and upon the strange destiny that seemed to connect me to that wretched creature whom I had encountered among the graves. The experience had marked me in ways that I could not fully comprehend at the time, implanting within my breast a sense of anxiety and expectation that never truly left me. It was as if the universe had whispered to me on that bleak afternoon that my life would never be entirely my own, and that I was bound by invisible threads to the fates of others, for good or ill. This intuition, vague though it was, proved to be not without foundation as the years unfolded.
